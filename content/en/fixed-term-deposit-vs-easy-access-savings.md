Cash you will not invest still has to live somewhere, and the choice usually comes down to two products: a **fixed-term deposit**, where you lock money away for a set period in exchange for a known rate, and an **easy-access savings account**, where you can withdraw at any time but the rate can change. When interest rates are moving, the gap between the two can be large, and picking the wrong one costs either interest or flexibility. The decision is not about which product is better. It is about matching each euro to the moment you will need it.

## How the two products work

### Fixed-term deposit

You agree to leave a sum with the bank for a fixed term, typically from three months to several years. The rate is set on day one and does not change. In return, access is restricted: depending on the product, early withdrawal is either impossible or allowed with a penalty, often the loss of part or all of the interest accrued.

### Easy-access savings account

Money can be moved in and out freely, usually to a linked current account. The rate is variable: the bank can raise or cut it, and introductory rates often fall back after a few months. Some accounts sit in between, with a notice period (for example 30 or 90 days) before you can withdraw.

## The trade-off in numbers

Use the simple-interest formula, which is how most deposits accrue over terms of a year or less:

**Interest = Principal × Annual rate × (Days ÷ 365)**

Take an illustrative case: €10,000 for 12 months, with a fixed-term rate of 3.0% and an easy-access rate of 2.0% (hypothetical figures, check what is actually on offer to you).

| Scenario | Calculation | Gross interest |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed term, held to maturity | 10,000 × 3.0% × 1 | €300 |
| Easy access, rate unchanged | 10,000 × 2.0% × 1 | €200 |
| Fixed term, broken at 6 months, interest forfeited | 0 | €0 |
| Easy access, withdrawn at 6 months | 10,000 × 2.0% × 0.5 | €100 |

The fixed deposit earns €100 more, but only if you never touch it. If there is a real chance you need the money early, the expected advantage shrinks fast, and with a full-forfeit penalty it can turn negative.

### Rate risk works in both directions

- **If rates fall**, the fixed deposit protects you: your rate is locked while easy-access rates are cut.
- **If rates rise**, the fixed deposit holds you back: you are stuck at the old rate until maturity.

Nobody can reliably forecast rates, so locking everything for a long term is a bet on one direction. Splitting the money reduces the cost of being wrong.

### Look at the real and net return

Two adjustments matter more than the headline rate:

- **Real return** = (1 + nominal rate) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. At 3.0% nominal and 2.0% inflation: 1.03 ÷ 1.02 − 1 ≈ 0.98%.
- **Net return** = gross interest × (1 − tax rate on interest). How interest is taxed, and at what rate, depends on where you live, so check the rules that apply to you.

## A practical method to decide

### 1. Separate the money by time horizon

- **Emergency fund**: belongs in easy access, or a short notice account at most. Its job is to be available, not to earn. If you have not sized it yet, start with our guide on [how much emergency fund you need and where to keep it](/en/blog/emergency-fund-how-much-where-to-keep-it).
- **Known expenses in 6 to 24 months** (a car, a deposit on a home, fees): a fixed term ending just before the date you need the money is a natural fit.
- **Money for 5+ years**: cash products are usually not the main tool here. For long horizons, see how an [ETF savings plan works](/en/blog/etf-savings-plan-how-it-works).

### 2. Consider a ladder

Instead of one large deposit, split the sum into several with staggered maturities. For example, €12,000 in four tranches of €3,000 maturing at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months. Every quarter one tranche becomes available: you can spend it or reinvest it at the prevailing rate. You give up a little yield compared with locking everything at the longest term, but you gain regular liquidity and average out rate changes.

### 3. Read the terms before the rate

- What exactly happens on early withdrawal: no access, partial penalty, or full loss of interest?
- Is the easy-access rate promotional, and for how long?
- How often is interest credited, and is it calculated on a 365- or 360-day basis?
- What happens at maturity: automatic renewal at an unknown rate, or payout?

### 4. Check deposit protection

Many countries run a deposit guarantee scheme that covers bank deposits up to a limit per depositor, per bank. Limits, eligible products and the treatment of foreign branches vary by jurisdiction. Before opening an account, confirm that the institution is authorised where you live, which scheme covers it, and what the limit is. If your balance exceeds the limit, spreading it across separate banking groups keeps every euro inside the coverage.

## The bottom line

Easy access is for money you might need at any time; fixed terms are for money with a known date. Map your cash to its horizon, ladder when you are unsure, check penalties and deposit protection, and always compare real, net returns rather than headline rates. **Want to see how a fixed rate compounds over several years?
