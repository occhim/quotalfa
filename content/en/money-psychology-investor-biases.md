The return you earn depends not only on what you buy but on what you do after buying it. And what you do is decided, more often than you think, by a part of the brain that evolved to escape predators, not to run a portfolio. Until you give it rules, it will decide for you — usually at the worst moment.

## Why we err predictably

Behavioural finance has shown that investor mistakes are not random: they repeat in the same direction. That is good news, because a predictable error can be prevented.

## The five costliest biases

### 1. Loss aversion

![Perceived value curve: losses weigh more than equal gains](/images/fig-money-biases-en.svg)

Work by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky indicates that a loss weighs roughly twice as much, psychologically, as a gain of the same size. The practical result: people hold losing positions hoping to “get back to even” and sell winners too early.

**Countermeasure:** decide your exit before entering, in writing.

### 2. Overconfidence

After a few good trades we credit skill and raise both frequency and size. In a widely cited study of tens of thousands of US household accounts, Brad Barber and Terrance Odean found that those who traded most earned net returns well below the market.

**Countermeasure:** keep a journal and measure results over at least a hundred trades before changing size.

### 3. Recency bias

We overweight what just happened. After a year of gains risk seems to vanish; after a crash it seems permanent. So exposure goes up at the highs and down at the lows.

**Countermeasure:** set your asset allocation in advance and rebalance on fixed dates, not on headlines.

### 4. Anchoring

Your purchase price becomes a reference point that means nothing to the market. “I’ll sell when it gets back to 40” is a decision based on the past, not on prospects.

**Countermeasure:** ask whether, not owning it today, you would buy that instrument at this price.

### 5. Herding

When everyone is talking about an investment, buying it feels safe. In practice, unanimous agreement usually arrives after much of the move has happened.

**Countermeasure:** impose 48 hours between idea and order for any purchase outside your plan.

## From spotting them to neutralising them

Knowing the biases is not enough: you spot them in others and keep falling for them. What works is rules that take the decision out of the emotional moment.

### Three automatisms

- **Automatic contributions.** A savings plan invests even when you are afraid.
- **Written rules.** How much you risk, when you exit, when you rebalance: decided with markets closed.
- **Fewer checks.** Look at a portfolio daily and you see more losses, and react more. For a long-term investment, once a month is plenty.

## The bottom line

You cannot remove emotions; you can stop them pressing the button. Every bias has the same cure: a rule set beforehand, when you are clear-headed, and followed afterwards, when you are not.
