Many ETFs come in two versions that hold exactly the same stocks: one pays dividends out to you, the other keeps them inside the fund and reinvests them. Same index, same holdings, often a similar cost. Yet over a few decades, picking the wrong one can cost more than a fee difference ever would. The reason is not the fund. It is what happens to the cash flow between the fund and your wallet.

## What the two share classes actually do

Every company in an index may pay dividends. The ETF collects them, and then it has two options.

- **Accumulating (Acc)**: the fund reinvests the dividends internally. You receive nothing in cash, but each share becomes worth more. The number of shares you own does not change.
- **Distributing (Dist)**: the fund pays the dividends out to investors, usually quarterly or twice a year. The share price drops by roughly the amount paid on the ex-dividend date, and the cash lands in your account.

Before costs and taxes, the total return is identical. An Acc share that grows by 6% and a Dist share that grows by 4% plus pays out 2% deliver the same result. The differences come from three places: tax, reinvestment discipline and what you need the money for.

## Tax drag: the difference that compounds

In many countries, dividends paid to you are taxed in the year you receive them, while gains inside a fund are taxed only when you sell. When that is the case, a distributing fund forces you to pay part of your return every year, and that money stops compounding.

A worked example makes the size of the effect visible. Take €10,000 invested for 20 years, a hypothetical total return of 6% a year, of which 2% comes from dividends, and an illustrative dividend tax of 25%. These are assumptions, not forecasts or the rate of any specific country.

- **Accumulating**: growth stays at 6%. Final value = 10,000 × 1.06^20 ≈ **€32,071**.
- **Distributing, dividends reinvested after tax**: each year you lose 2% × 25% = 0.5%, so effective growth is 5.5%. Final value = 10,000 × 1.055^20 ≈ **€29,178**.

The gap is about €2,894, before any tax due when you eventually sell. In the accumulating case, a larger unrealised gain is taxed at the exit, so the true after-tax difference is smaller than this figure suggests. It remains real, though, because tax deferred is tax that keeps working for you in the meantime.

### Why this depends entirely on where you live

The example only holds under one specific tax logic. Real systems vary a lot:

- Some countries tax accumulating funds every year on a deemed or notional income, which removes most of the deferral advantage.
- Some offer tax-free allowances on dividends, or tax-sheltered accounts where neither class is taxed until withdrawal, making the choice nearly irrelevant.

Before choosing, check how your country treats fund distributions, accumulating funds and the account type you are using. Official tax authority guidance or a local professional is the right source.

## Reinvestment discipline and costs

Even where taxes are neutral, accumulating funds have a behavioural edge. Reinvestment is automatic and immediate, with no transaction fee and no temptation. With a distributing fund, the cash sits in your account until you decide what to do. Small payouts are easy to forget, spend or leave idle for months.

There is also a practical cost: reinvesting a €40 dividend by hand can trigger a minimum commission that eats a meaningful share of it. If you run a regular investment plan, you can solve this by adding the dividends to your next scheduled purchase. The approach is covered in our guide to [ETF savings plans](/en/blog/etf-savings-plan-how-it-works).

## When distributing makes sense

Distributing ETFs are not a mistake. They fit specific situations:

1. **You need income now.** In retirement or when living off a portfolio, regular payouts cover expenses without selling shares.
2. **Your tax system favours it.** Where dividends benefit from an allowance or accumulating funds are taxed on deemed income, Dist can be equal or better.
3. **You value the psychological effect.** Some investors stay invested through downturns more easily when they see cash arriving. That has value, as long as you are aware of the [behavioural biases](/en/blog/money-psychology-investor-biases) behind it.

Remember that a dividend is not extra return. It is part of the total return moved from the share price to your account. A fund paying 3% is not better than one paying 1% for that reason alone.

## A quick decision framework

| Your situation | Usually fits better |
| --- | --- |
| Building wealth, long horizon, dividends taxed yearly | Accumulating |
| Living off the portfolio | Distributing |
| Tax-sheltered account with no annual tax | Either: compare cost and tracking |
| Accumulating funds taxed on deemed income | Compare after-tax outcomes case by case |
| Small portfolio, high minimum commissions | Accumulating |

## The bottom line

Accumulating and distributing ETFs hold the same assets. What separates them is when you pay tax and whether reinvestment happens automatically. For long-term wealth building, accumulating classes often win on compounding and simplicity. For income or under specific tax rules, distributing classes can be the better tool. Check your local rules, run your own numbers and choose based on what you need the money to do.
