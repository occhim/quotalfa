The October US inflation report, published on November 10, set the tone for the rest of the month. This week the Federal Reserve's minutes and fresh consumer data added to the picture, and the next few weeks bring the releases that will shape the Fed's December decision. This recap covers only what has been published by the official sources, with dates, and explains how to read each release instead of guessing where prices go next.

## What the week's data said

### The inflation backdrop: October CPI

According to the US Bureau of Labor Statistics (release of November 10, 2026), the Consumer Price Index rose **0.4%** in October on a seasonally adjusted basis, and core CPI (excluding food and energy) rose **0.3%**.

One detail is easy to miss: the BLS did **not** publish a 12-month change for October. The agency did not collect survey data for October 2025 because of the lapse in federal appropriations, so there is no base month for the annual comparison. Headlines quoting a precise annual figure for October CPI are therefore estimates, not official data.

To put a monthly number in perspective, annualize it yourself:

`Annualized rate = (1 + monthly change)^12 − 1`

A 0.3% core reading gives (1.003)^12 − 1 ≈ **3.7%** per year. One month says little on its own, but this is why monthly core prints near 0.3% worry a central bank with a 2% target.

### Fed minutes: the debate is about how much, not whether

On November 18 the Federal Reserve released the minutes of the October 27–28 FOMC meeting. The key points:

- The Committee held the federal funds target range at **3.75%–4.00%**, with a **10–2** vote. Governors Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack dissented in favor of a 25 basis point increase.
- Almost all participants judged that **further increases** in the target range would likely be appropriate under their baseline outlook.
- A couple of participants said an increase **larger than 25 basis points** could be considered at the December meeting if inflation data came in unfavorably.
- Decisions remain data-dependent, which is exactly why the next releases matter.

### Consumers: October retail sales

The US Census Bureau's advance estimate, published on November 17, showed retail sales up **0.2%** in October after a 0.8% gain in September. Excluding motor vehicles and parts, sales rose **0.6%**. Spending held up despite tighter financial conditions, an element that supports the hawkish side of the debate.

### How to read the PCE release

PCE inflation usually differs from CPI because it uses different weights (health care, for example, weighs more in PCE) and a broader basket. When reading it:

1. **Start with core PCE month-on-month**, then annualize it with the formula above.
2. **Compare with expectations**, not with zero. Markets react to the surprise, the gap between the actual figure and the consensus.
3. **Check revisions** to previous months: a revised prior month can matter as much as the new number.
4. **Read spending and income too**: strong real spending combined with sticky inflation would reinforce the case made in the minutes.
