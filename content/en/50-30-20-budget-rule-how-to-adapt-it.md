The end of the year is when many people take stock. Bonuses or extra pay are on their way, so are holiday expenses, and the gaps in the past twelve months become hard to ignore. It is a good moment to adopt a simple structure. The 50/30/20 rule is the best known, but applied literally it often fails to add up against real salaries and real rents. Here is how it works and how to adapt it to your own numbers, wherever you live.

## What the 50/30/20 rule is

The rule was popularised by the 2005 book *All Your Worth* by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi. Net income is split into three buckets:

- **50% needs**: costs you cannot avoid (housing, utilities, groceries, commuting, loan payments, mandatory insurance).
- **30% wants**: anything you could cut without serious consequences (eating out, subscriptions, travel, hobbies).
- **20% savings and debt**: emergency fund, investing, paying down debt early.

Its strength is simplicity: three numbers instead of twenty line items. Its weakness is that the percentages are a starting point, not a law.

## Step one: your real monthly net income

Your take-home pay in an ordinary month is often not your average monthly income. Depending on your country and contract, you may receive extra monthly payments, an annual bonus, profit-sharing or commission that lands only a few times a year. Budget on a normal month's pay and you underestimate your income; treat those payments as spare cash and your budget breaks every year.

The fix is to think annually:

**Monthly equivalent net = total annual net ÷ 12**

Example with €1,600 net per month plus one extra month's pay at year end:

| Item | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Annual net | 1,600 × 13 | €20,800 |
| Monthly equivalent | 20,800 ÷ 12 | €1,733 |

Apply the percentages to the monthly equivalent. The difference from your ordinary pay (€133 a month in this example) arrives as a lump sum, so assign it in advance: annual bills such as insurance, local taxes and maintenance, plus savings. The same logic works for a bonus or commission income: add up the whole year and divide by twelve.

## Why 50% for needs often falls short

On €1,733, half is €866.50. Now take a realistic set of fixed costs for someone renting in a city:

| Item | Monthly cost |
|---|---|
| Rent | €650 |
| Utilities | €150 |
| Groceries | €300 |
| Commuting | €150 |
| **Total needs** | **€1,250** |

Needs come to 1,250 ÷ 1,733 = **72%** of income. Taken literally, the rule has failed before you start. That is not a personal failing: with typical pay and city rents, a 50% needs share is often out of reach in the short term.

## How to recalibrate the split

### 1. Measure, don't guess

Go through the last three months of bank statements and tag every outflow as a need, a want or savings. Divide annual costs by twelve and add them in. Only then do you know your baseline.

### 2. Pay yourself first

The 20% is the bucket that matters most, because it is the only one that builds wealth. If it is out of reach, choose a lower figure but automate it: a transfer scheduled for payday. In the example, with needs at 72%, you have 1,733 − 1,250 = €483 left. One workable split:

| Bucket | Share | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Needs | 72% | €1,250 |
| Savings | 15% | €260 |
| Wants | 13% | €223 |

The result is 72/13/15: far from the textbook version, but grounded in reality and sustainable.

### 3. Let wants absorb the shocks

When needs rise, squeeze wants, not savings. When needs fall (a renegotiated energy contract, a transit pass instead of a car), move at least part of the saving into the savings bucket.

### 4. Reset the target once a year

A pay rise or a loan that ends is your chance to move closer to 50/30/20. A practical rule: send at least half of every increase in net pay to savings.

## Useful variations

- **High-interest debt**: while you carry it, the 20% goes to paying it off first.
- **Incomplete emergency fund**: fill it before investing. See [how much you really need and where to keep it](/en/blog/emergency-fund-how-much-where-to-keep-it).
- **Already saving 20%**: the next step is investing it consistently, for example through an [ETF savings plan](/en/blog/etf-savings-plan-how-it-works).

## The bottom line

The 50/30/20 rule is a compass, not a constraint. Work out your monthly equivalent net including bonuses and any extra pay, measure what your needs actually cost, automate your savings and use wants as the adjustment margin. The right percentages are the ones you can stick to for years.
