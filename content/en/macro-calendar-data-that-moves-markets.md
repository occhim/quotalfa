Every month there are a few minutes when spreads widen, stops get run and a clean chart turns unreadable. It is not random: a macroeconomic release has just hit. Trade without checking the calendar and you hand your risk to a number you did not even know you were waiting for. Ten minutes on a Sunday evening prevents it.

## Why a release moves prices

Markets do not react to the figure itself but to the **surprise**: the gap between the published number and the analyst consensus. High but expected inflation moves little; inflation slightly above expectations can move a lot.

### The thread that ties it together: rates

Almost every major release matters because it shifts expectations for central bank decisions. Higher expected rates weigh on equities and bonds and support the currency; lower expected rates generally do the opposite.

## The five to mark

![Typical cadence of macro releases through the month](/images/fig-macro-calendar-en.svg)

### 1. Inflation

The consumer price index (CPI in the United States, HICP in the euro area) measures how fast the cost of living rises. Watch the “core” measure, which strips out energy and food: it is the one central banks reason on.

### 2. The labour market

The US employment report (Non-Farm Payrolls) usually lands on the first Friday of the month at 8:30 a.m. Eastern Time. Three numbers matter: jobs added, the unemployment rate and wage growth.

### 3. Central bank decisions

The Federal Reserve and the ECB each hold eight monetary policy meetings a year. The rate decision is often priced in already: what moves markets is the statement, the projections and the press conference.

### 4. PMI surveys

Monthly surveys of purchasing managers. Above 50 signals expansion, below 50 contraction. They are among the first data available for the month just ended, so they lead the direction of the economy.

### 5. GDP

It measures overall growth and is published quarterly. It arrives late compared with other indicators, but a large surprise can change the narrative for weeks.

## How to use it if you trade

### Before the release

- Note the week’s high-impact releases and the markets they affect.
- If you hold positions, decide beforehand whether to reduce, close or keep them with a stop suited to the expected volatility.
- Avoid opening new positions in the minutes before the number.

### During and after

In the first minutes spreads widen and execution worsens: slippage can exceed the risk you calculated. Let the first move play out and judge direction only once price settles around a level.

## How to use it if you invest long term

For someone running a savings plan, the macro calendar is good for one thing: understanding why the portfolio moves, without changing the programme. A disappointing figure is no reason to pause contributions.

## The bottom line

Five releases, one rule: the surprise against expectations matters, not the number. Knowing when they come out does not tell you where the market will go, but it tells you when your risk is higher than usual.
