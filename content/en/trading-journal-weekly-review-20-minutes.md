Most traders keep some kind of journal. Far fewer ever read it back. The log grows, the screenshots pile up, and the same mistakes repeat because nobody stops to look for them. Trading psychology is not fixed by willpower; it is fixed by noticing patterns early, while they are still cheap. A short, fixed weekly review is the simplest way to do that, and the weekend, with markets closed and no open decisions, is the right moment.

This routine takes about 20 minutes. It is not meant to grade your profit and loss. It is meant to answer one question: **did I trade my plan, and if not, why?**

## What your journal needs before the review

A review is only as good as what you recorded during the week. For each trade, the minimum is:

- Date, instrument, direction, entry, stop and exit
- Planned risk in account currency and the result in **R** (multiples of the initial risk)
- The setup name, taken from your written plan
- A yes/no for "followed the plan" (entry, stop, size, exit)
- One line on your state of mind before entering

R is the key field. If you risked €50 and made €100, the trade is +2R; if you lost the full €50, it is -1R. R removes account size from the picture and makes weeks comparable.

## The 20-minute routine

### Minutes 0-5: the numbers

Compute four figures, nothing more:

| Metric | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Net result in R | Sum of all trades' R |
| Win rate | Winning trades ÷ total trades |
| Average win and average loss (in R) | Sum of winning R ÷ wins; sum of losing R ÷ losses |
| Plan adherence | Trades that followed the plan ÷ total trades |

Example: 10 trades, 4 winners averaging +1.8R and 6 losers averaging -0.9R. Net result = (4 × 1.8) − (6 × 0.9) = 7.2 − 5.4 = **+1.8R**. Win rate 40%. If 7 of the 10 trades followed the plan, adherence is 70%.

One week is far too small a sample to judge a strategy, so treat these numbers as a description, not a verdict. The concept behind them is covered in our guide on [expectancy, risk-reward and win rate](/en/blog/expectancy-risk-reward-win-rate).

### Minutes 5-12: split the trades in two

Separate trades that followed the plan from trades that did not, and compute net R for each group.

This is the most useful split in the whole review. A losing week where every trade followed the rules is normal variance. A winning week built on rule breaks is a warning: it rewards behaviour that will eventually hurt you. If the off-plan group is consistently negative, you have found where your money leaks, and it has nothing to do with the strategy.

### Minutes 12-17: find the pattern

Look only at the off-plan trades and ask:

1. **What broke?** Early entry, moved stop, oversized position, skipped exit, revenge trade.
2. **When?** Time of day, after a loss, after a big win, near the end of the week.
3. **What was the state of mind?** Boredom, fear of missing out, frustration, overconfidence.

You are looking for repetition, not a single bad trade. Two moved stops after a losing trade in the same week is a pattern worth naming. If stop discipline is the issue, revisit [where a stop loss belongs and where it does not](/en/blog/stop-loss-placement-where-to-put-it).

### Minutes 17-20: one change, written down

Pick **one** adjustment for next week and write it as a concrete rule, not an intention:

- Weak: "Be more patient."
- Strong: "After two consecutive losses, no new trades until the next session."

One rule is enough. Changing five things at once makes it impossible to know what helped. Next Sunday, the first thing you check is whether you kept it.

## Common mistakes in weekly reviews

- **Judging by P&L only.** Money results are noisy over a few trades; process results are not.
- **Rewriting history.** Record your state of mind before the trade, not after you know the outcome.
- **Changing the strategy every week.** A rule change is behavioural; a strategy change needs a much larger sample and proper testing.
- **Skipping bad weeks.** The weeks you least want to review are the ones that teach the most.
- **Making it too long.** A two-hour review is rarely repeated. Twenty minutes, every week, beats a perfect review done once.

A reminder on risk: if you trade leveraged products such as CFDs or forex, most retail accounts lose money. A journal does not change that, but it shows you clearly whether your own behaviour is making things worse.

## The bottom line

A weekly review turns a trade log into feedback. Compute four numbers, split on-plan from off-plan trades, name the pattern behind the breaks and commit to one written rule. Repeat it every weekend and the journal starts doing its real job: making your decisions more consistent over time.
